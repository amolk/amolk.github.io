Indian classical music, attempted twice, thirteen years apart and with no shared code.

## The current build

Hindustani vocal music is learned by imitation: the teacher sings a phrase, the student sings it back, and the cycle repeats until the shape is right. Ragafied turns an archival recording into exactly that loop. It separates the performance into vocal and accompaniment, proposes phrase boundaries across the whole recording, and then plays each phrase twice - once as the teacher sang it, and once with the vocal removed so the tanpura and harmonium hold the same window open for you to answer into. Do nothing and the phrase repeats. Tap to move on.

Everything a singer needs to bend is independent of everything else. You can move the tonic to your own register without changing the tempo, slow a phrase down without lowering its pitch, and step through a graded tempo ladder as a passage becomes playable. Turn controls make the guru turn and the learner turn explicit rather than implicit, and loops can be dragged tighter around the two seconds that are actually giving you trouble.

The visual feedback is deliberately not a score. The teacher's pitch contour is drawn in the swar notation of the raga; yours is drawn live against it as you sing; detector confidence controls opacity, so uncertainty looks uncertain and gaps stay gaps instead of being interpolated into invented notes. Octave displacement between a male recording and a female voice is folded away so the two contours overlay as one melodic shape. There is no composite grade, because a number would be read as a verdict long before it deserves to be one.

## The motion between notes

What a singer has to copy is not really a sequence of notes; it is the motion between them - the slide, the shake, the flick that carries one note into the next. The app reads that motion directly. It separates a phrase into sustained notes and the moving edges between them, and offers each edge a name from the vocabulary the tradition already uses: meend, gamak, khatka, andolan. A workbench draws the annotated contour, lists what it found as hypotheses rather than verdicts, and puts the detector's own thresholds under your hand, so a label is something you can argue with instead of something handed down.

Reading the motion changes what slowing down should mean. Playing a phrase uniformly slower stretches the sustained notes you could already hear perfectly well, and leaves the ornament - which happens in a fraction of a second - still too fast to imitate. So the slow-down is uneven: the moving parts stretch far harder than the held ones, eased between the two so nothing lurches, and the pitch contour and the moving cursor follow that same uneven clock.

## Listening in a real room

The hard part is not drawing pitch - it is hearing the singer at all. A learner practicing without headphones is singing over their own speakers, and the accompaniment leaking back into the microphone is louder than they are.

So the app measures the room instead of guessing at it. Because the accompaniment is generated in the browser, its exact signal is known ahead of the microphone, and the two share one clock. Before a session, the lesson's own accompaniment plays into the room with nobody singing; the app captures it, fits the speaker-and-room path from that capture, and then validates the fit against a second, held-out capture rather than trusting the first one. Neural pitch detection runs over the teacher's vocal ahead of time and in the browser for the learner, behind a short fixed lag that buys accuracy a purely causal detector cannot reach.

That work produced the constraint the whole listening path is now built around: an offline lab can subtract the room very aggressively because a person listens to the result and judges it, but a neural pitch detector is far less forgiving. Subtract too deeply and you erase the voice's own harmonics, leaving narrow tonal residue that *looks* like pitch. The live path therefore stops short on purpose and leaves a measured fraction of the raw microphone in the analysis signal. Cancelling the room too well is its own failure mode.

Around the practice space sit a timeline studio for authoring lessons from a recording, and a design system - calm instrument surfaces, a single accent colour used only as a signal - so the interface reads as an instrument rather than a dashboard.

## A service, not a demo

The practice space now sits inside a working product: an account, a private library of your own recordings, lessons authored from those recordings, and sharing that is explicit about who may open what.

It has no permanent roles, and that is a deliberate choice. A student following someone else's lesson, a teacher turning a recording into one, and a singer uploading material purely for their own practice are three needs rather than three kinds of account. One account covers any of them, and the interface reshapes around the task at hand instead of asking anyone to declare an identity at signup - so a teacher can practice someone else's lesson and a student can build a private one.

Access is a single policy, checked in a single place. Every page, audio file, pitch track and practice session passes the same gate, because an access rule reimplemented per surface is how a media library eventually leaks. Visibility and permission stay separate questions: a link is a way to find a lesson, never a way past a rule about who can open it. Recordings stay private until their owner publishes a lesson made from them; a lesson cannot be published while its content, its audio or its rights are unresolved; and nothing reaches the public catalogue before review, while direct links keep working while that review is pending - so approval gates discovery rather than access. Moderation is auditable by construction: each decision records who made it, why, and what changed. Operators can review public content and still cannot open anyone's private recordings.

The deployment is described as infrastructure rather than assembled by hand - a deliberately small production footprint defined in code, and one image carrying the site, the practice space, the studio and the background workers.

## A face for it

The public page is a painted scene rather than a product shot: a singer learning beside a tanpura, with the practice space appearing inside it as a small working preview - a phrase, a speed control, and the two pitch traces answering each other - instead of a marquee. The words underneath are deliberately mechanical about what happens: a short phrase plays with its original accompaniment, the singer drops out, and you sing the same phrase over the instruments. Hear, sing, compare.

It is aimed at one thing first. Hindustani classical and Indian film music are where listening and repeating have always been the method, so that is what the product commits to; the same studio-and-player model could later carry dance, language, or anything else learned by repeating after someone, but that is a direction rather than a claim. The visual language moves as one system across the site, the practice space, the studio and the calibration workbench - calm instrument surfaces, a single accent colour used only as a signal.

## The 2013 build

The first attempt was a Rails 3.2 SaaS scaffold shaped around the same idea: `Raga`, `Guru`, `Lesson` and `Audio` models wired up alongside users and roles, with Devise and CanCan for auth, Bootstrap and Haml on the front end, and RSpec and Cucumber behind it. It is a fair snapshot of how a full Rails application got bootstrapped in 2013, and of an idea that had to wait for the browser to catch up with it.
