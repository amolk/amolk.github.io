I first built Ragafied in 2012-13, around an idea that has not changed since: Hindustani vocal music is learned by imitation, so a recording of a great singer should become something you can sing back to, one phrase at a time. The browser of the time could not carry that idea. Its audio support was young and uneven from one browser to the next, custom sound processing could not run off the page's main thread, there was no way to run near-native code or a neural model inside a web page, and microphone access was patchy. What I could build then was the scaffold around the idea - accounts, ragas, gurus, lessons - and not the instrument at its centre.

The 2026 version is that instrument, and it runs at [ragafied.com](https://ragafied.com).

## What it is built on

- **AI models.** Neural source separation lifts the lead vocal away from the accompaniment. A neural pitch detector reads the teacher's melody ahead of time and the learner's voice live in the browser. A neural beat tracker finds the pulse across the whole recording. The heavy models run on cloud graphics hardware that scales to zero when nothing is waiting.
- **Offline signal processing.** Every upload goes through a pipeline on the server - separation, rhythm, pitch, phrases, playback preparation - with each result remembered by the audio's own fingerprint, so no work is ever done twice.
- **Online signal processing.** In the browser, in real time: key and tempo move independently of each other, changes cross over mid-phrase without a gap, the room is measured and the accompaniment cancelled out of the microphone, and a chosen tempo and key are rendered ahead and kept on the device.
- **Algorithms written for this music.** A pitch-and-tempo processor that rebuilds a slowed voice from the singer's own pitch periods, so a phrase at a fifth of its speed still sounds like a person singing. Phrase boundaries placed where the singer actually breathed. Ornaments - meend, gamak, khatka, andolan - read from the motion between notes, and a slow-down that stretches those ornaments far more than the held notes around them.

## Designed as an instrument

The part I am proudest of is how it feels to use. The homepage of ragafied.com opens on the real player rather than a picture of it, so the first thing a visitor does is press play, slow a phrase down and drop the key. The player is built for a thumb: tempo, key and mode are labels you drag through, with the value you are about to choose shown large in the middle of the player; the music and the voice mute on a tap and set their level on a drag; a point of light circling the play button says the sound is being prepared, and a tap cancels it. It comes in four looks, plus a look of its own for any track whose owner designs one, and it can fill the whole screen. The pitch graph is drawn in the swar notation of the raga, and there is deliberately no score. The sections below describe each of these in detail.

## One object, many ways to use it

The product settled on a single noun: a **track**. You give it a recording you are entitled to use, it separates the lead vocal from everything behind it, and what comes back is one object you can sing along to. Everything else is a capability of that same object rather than a separate mode - sing the whole song with the original vocal turned down, pull a range tight around the passage that is actually hard, let the app propose sections as shortcuts, watch your pitch against the original, or follow guidance a teacher authored on top of it. A lesson is no longer a thing you must build before you can sing; it is teacher-authored guidance that uses a track.

That reordering matters more than it sounds. The earlier build asked you to author a lesson first, which meant the fastest path to singing ran through an editing tool. Now the fastest path is upload, wait for separation, and sing - and the practice apparatus below is still there when you want it. An audio file is the usual starting point, and a video file works too because the app takes the audio out of it. Recordings up to an hour and a half are accepted, because a full Hindustani classical performance is rarely short.

Sharing is deliberately narrow. A track's owner shares one track at a time through a link they can revoke, and revoking it stops playback that is already running rather than merely hiding the next click - because a saved link or a playlist entry is exactly how a media library usually leaks. A track shared with a member shows up in their own library the first time they open the link, and leaves it when the link is revoked. What they can do with it there is play it; where it came from stays with its owner. If they want to make it their own - rename it, re-run the separation, edit its phrases - they copy it into their library, and the copy is a track of theirs with every setting carried over and no second copy of the audio behind it, because the service stores each distinct recording once however many libraries it sits in.

## The practice space

Hindustani vocal music is learned by imitation: the teacher sings a phrase, the student sings it back, and the cycle repeats until the shape is right. Ragafied turns an archival recording into exactly that loop. It separates the performance into vocal and accompaniment, proposes phrase boundaries across the whole recording, and then plays each phrase twice - once as the teacher sang it, and once with the vocal removed so the tanpura and harmonium hold the same window open for you to answer into. Do nothing and the phrase repeats. Tap to move on. How the loop runs is a preset you pick by name - listen, listen three times, sing, sing three times, or alternate the two - and the count-in and tail that give a sing turn its context play only around the sing turn - before every sing turn the playhead jumps back to, never before a phrase you are only listening to. The loop button is a readout of what the preset is about to do, lit while the current turn will repeat and dark when it will move on, and a tap pins it the other way for the rest of that phrase. The music and the original voice each have a separate balance for the listening turn and the singing turn, so the singer can be at full volume while you listen and a quiet reference while you sing. The controls are built for a thumb: the music and vocal buttons mute on a tap and set their level on a drag through a short ladder of stops that is fine near silence and coarse near full, mode, tempo and key are labels you drag through their options so nothing reflows under your finger, and whichever control you are dragging shows its options large in the middle of the player for as long as you hold it, the value that is about to apply biggest and its neighbours fading away above and below. A full-screen button gives the player the whole display, edge to edge; with the pitch graph open the graph takes the extra height and shows the same notes larger, and with it closed the waveform grows into the space instead. While the player is still preparing sound the play button shows a small point of light circling its edge - tap it and the wait is cancelled. If the next phrase is not ready the moment the current one ends, the player holds with that same light and starts as soon as it is, rather than stopping and asking you to press play again.

The proposed boundaries are a starting point, not a verdict. A waveform editor lets you split a phrase, merge two, or slide a shared boundary while the passage loops under your hands, listening to the whole track or the vocal alone, with undo all the way back. The first proposal comes from where the voice itself pauses: the app listens to the vocal alone, finds the rests, prefers phrases of a singable length, and moves a boundary onto a nearby beat only when the beat falls inside the rest - so phrases start where the singer breathed, and a cut it was forced to make without a real pause is marked for you to review rather than passed off as one.

Everything a singer needs to bend is independent of everything else. You can move the tonic to your own register without changing the tempo, slow a phrase down without lowering its pitch, and step through a graded tempo ladder as a passage becomes playable. Every stem - the voice and the accompaniment alike - now passes through one processor, written for this. It keeps a note's attack and its ringing apart from its sustained pitch, and when the voice is slowed far down it rebuilds each voiced moment from the singer's own pitch periods, placed by how each period resembles the last rather than by where the waveform peaks, so the harmonics stay lined up instead of smearing into a flanged shimmer; at the original key and tempo it copies the recording untouched. A phrase can play at a fifth of its tempo, or a full octave away, and still sound like a singer; the voice keeps its own resonance as it moves key, so a lowered vocal does not turn into a different, larger person. The processor was signed off by ear on a phone - slow passages, a shifted key, a seek from the lock screen - before it shipped. Changing key or tempo while a phrase is playing no longer stops it: the player prepares the new sound a little ahead of where you are and crosses over to it at a fixed moment on the audio clock, so a change you make mid-phrase is a change you hear, not a gap. The engine keeps its own housekeeping bounded - a run of student repeats cannot exhaust it, and on a machine that cannot keep up it offers a lighter listening mode rather than stalling - and play, pause and every jump fade instead of clicking. Turn controls make the guru turn and the learner turn explicit rather than implicit, and loops can be dragged tighter around the two seconds that are actually giving you trouble. The habits of a practice hour - a count-in before the phrase, an audible cue where it starts and ends - are settings you make once and keep, not something to rebuild for every lesson. Moving a voice several notes used to leave a second, detuned voice behind it; the shifter now follows the singer's own voice to keep its character as the pitch moves, and the correction stays under your hand for the passage where the automatic choice is wrong.

The visual feedback is deliberately not a score. The teacher's pitch contour is drawn in the swar notation of the raga; yours is drawn live against it as you sing; detector confidence controls opacity, so uncertainty looks uncertain and gaps stay gaps instead of being interpolated into invented notes. Octave displacement between a male recording and a female voice is folded away so the two contours overlay as one melodic shape. The graph has no toolbar to get in the way: drag its note axis to zoom, tap it to switch between sargam and Western note names, and scrub along the contour to hear the note under your finger held rather than warbled. There is no composite grade, because a number would be read as a verdict long before it deserves to be one.

## The motion between notes

What a singer has to copy is not really a sequence of notes; it is the motion between them - the slide, the shake, the flick that carries one note into the next. The app reads that motion directly. It separates a phrase into sustained notes and the moving edges between them, and offers each edge a name from the vocabulary the tradition already uses: meend, gamak, khatka, andolan. A workbench draws the annotated contour, lists what it found as hypotheses rather than verdicts, and puts the detector's own thresholds under your hand, so a label is something you can argue with instead of something handed down.

Pitch alone cannot settle every one of those labels. A note slid into and a note struck again can trace almost the same line, and the difference is in the attack rather than in the melody. So each moment of the teacher's track now also carries a measure of how sharply the sound restarts there, taken over a far shorter window than pitch needs and scored relative to the phrase it sits in, so it means the same thing across recordings mastered decades apart.

Reading the motion changes what slowing down should mean. Playing a phrase uniformly slower stretches the sustained notes you could already hear perfectly well, and leaves the ornament - which happens in a fraction of a second - still too fast to imitate. So the slow-down is uneven: the moving parts stretch far harder than the held ones, eased between the two so nothing lurches, and the pitch contour and the moving cursor follow that same uneven clock. And when a single note is the thing that has to be got right, the sound can dwell on it, replaying that one moment while the cursor holds still, instead of moving on because the clock says so.

## Listening in a real room

The hard part is not drawing pitch - it is hearing the singer at all. A learner practicing without headphones is singing over their own speakers, and the accompaniment leaking back into the microphone is louder than they are.

So the app measures the room instead of guessing at it. Because the accompaniment is generated in the browser, its exact signal is known ahead of the microphone, and the two share one clock. Before a session, the lesson's own accompaniment plays into the room with nobody singing; the app captures it, fits the speaker-and-room path from that capture, and then validates the fit against a second, held-out capture rather than trusting the first one. Neural pitch detection runs over the teacher's vocal ahead of time and in the browser for the learner, behind a short fixed lag that buys accuracy a purely causal detector cannot reach.

That work produced the constraint the whole listening path is now built around: an offline lab can subtract the room very aggressively because a person listens to the result and judges it, but a neural pitch detector is far less forgiving. Subtract too deeply and you erase the voice's own harmonics, leaving narrow tonal residue that *looks* like pitch. The live path therefore stops short on purpose and leaves a measured fraction of the raw microphone in the analysis signal. Cancelling the room too well is its own failure mode.

The cancellation also reports on itself, rather than working silently and leaving you to guess. It says whether it has locked onto the room yet, how confident that lock is, and how much of the accompaniment it is actually removing - so a microphone that cannot hear you looks different from a room the app has not finished measuring.

Around the practice space sit a timeline studio for authoring lessons from a recording, and a design system - calm instrument surfaces, a single accent colour used only as a signal - so the interface reads as an instrument rather than a dashboard.

## A service, not a demo

The practice space now sits inside a working product: an account, a private library of your own recordings, lessons authored from those recordings, and sharing that is explicit about who may open what.

Signing in and creating an account are one page, because the ways in are the same for both - a link sent to your email, a password, or a Google account - and a newcomer should not have to work out which of two doors is theirs; the site simply says *Get started*. It has no permanent roles, and that is a deliberate choice. A student following someone else's lesson, a teacher turning a recording into one, and a singer uploading material purely for their own practice are three needs rather than three kinds of account. One account covers any of them, and the interface reshapes around the task at hand instead of asking anyone to declare an identity at signup - so a teacher can practice someone else's lesson and a student can build a private one.

Audio itself is delivered under that same policy rather than beside it. The original recording and the separated stems it produces stay private and are never served; playback receives short, lossy, individually authorized pieces instead of a file, each one re-checked against the same rule, with no whole-file path anywhere to fall back to and nothing offered as a download. The honest framing is the one the design document uses: this is friction against casual copying, not a guarantee - a browser that can play audio can always capture it, and the product does not claim otherwise.

The player was then rebuilt to load only what it is about to play. Startup and memory no longer grow with the length of the recording: a bounded queue of small fetches feeds a rolling window into the audio engine, a seek or a cancel discards work that is no longer needed, and a chunk that arrives incomplete becomes a visible, recoverable error instead of a silent gap. The one deliberate exception to online-only playback is for performances: the owner of a track can save it for offline use, and the saved copy plays without a network for a short, fixed period before it has to be renewed - if that period runs out mid-song, the song finishes. It was verified by saving a track, cutting the network, and playing it through. On an Android phone the player now keeps going with the screen off, answers the lock-screen controls, and picks up again after another app takes the audio; the track you are practicing is cached quietly in the background so it can be kept for offline use. Safari is still not claimed, and neither is the musical quality of pitch and tempo changes on a phone until that has been listened to on a real song.

The next pass taught it to keep what it has already made. A practice loop plays the same phrase many times, and a dense lesson - eight separated stems, a teacher turn and a student turn, each at its own tempo and key - used to render each of them from scratch every cycle, which is what stalled it on phones. Now a finished render is cached and reused: a second voice can borrow the teacher's rendered audio while the first is still playing it, the student's turn waits for the teacher's audio instead of racing it, a repeated lead-in reuses the first one's output, and when every rendering worker is busy the overflow goes through one bounded fallback rather than waiting forever. The signal processing itself got leaner too - fewer copies, smaller lookup tables, an envelope convolution that picks its block size by the work it will cost - all of it checked sample for sample against the previous output. Under heavy simulated throttling, a lesson that took nine seconds to start now starts in under three and plays through a full minute at half speed without a pause; and on a real phone the same lesson now survives a seek from the lock screen with the screen off, which is where it used to fall silent. The same idea now reaches the phone itself: once you pick a tempo and key, the whole track is rendered at that setting in the background and kept on the device in compressed form, so steady playback does no signal processing at all and returning to a setting you already used starts at once. Playback also rides out a weak signal - a practice session on a walk no longer stops every few seconds when the connection drops, because a brief network failure is treated as a gap to wait through rather than as a reason to stop, while a real change in who may hear the track still stops it.

The player's engine has been rebuilt around one idea: each voice is a track on a timeline, and every control - play, pause, seek, loop, a new key or tempo - is a move on that timeline. It was built in stages, each measured on a phone before the next began, and every track now plays on it: the practice sequence of phrases, turns, loops and ranges, the scrubber, the pitch graph, saved offline copies, and controls from the lock screen. A jump to audio already on the device is heard within a tenth of a second, the voice and the accompaniment start and stay together to within a fraction of a millisecond, a new key or tempo is rendered on the phone itself and playing in under a second, and a setting heard before replays almost at once because its rendered audio stays on the device.

A new track no longer needs anyone to drive it through those tools. Drop a file and the same engines the editors use run in a fixed order - separation, rhythm, pitch, phrases, playback preparation - with every parameter written down, each stage showing not started, in progress, done or failed, and any finished stage open for inspection while the later ones are still running. Each running task says how long it expects to take and counts down honestly - when work runs over, it says it is still working rather than showing a false zero - and finished or stopped work stays visible as an outcome instead of vanishing, with the whole processing history sortable, searchable and actionable. The same library is open to software as well as to people: every page has a plain-text form a program can read, every action the browser can take has a documented contract, and an assistant can be given a revocable, scoped key to run those actions - including the phrase, pitch and rhythm editors - through the same drafts, undo and save paths a person uses, with the same ownership rules applied. What arrives at the end is a review, not a published track: the owner listens, corrects what needs correcting, and approves a specific version before the standalone player or a shared link will serve it. Approval freezes that version, so later edits never change what a listener already has until the owner approves again. The heavy stages now run on graphics hardware when it is available and fall back to ordinary processors when it is not, workers stay warm between jobs instead of starting cold for each one, and every release checks that the exact image and queue it was verified against are the ones actually running. When the cloud itself cannot start a graphics worker - a capacity outage in the zones the fleet was using, which happened - the queue no longer fails every waiting job after a few tries: it waits with increasing patience, sends one probe worker at a time to see whether capacity is back, tells the operator why it is waiting and when it will try again, and the fleet now reaches every zone that offers its hardware rather than two. The operator console reports on that processing as a whole - how long each kind of task takes, how much of it ran on graphics hardware, where the failures cluster - rather than one job at a time. Nothing is analyzed twice: every result is remembered by the audio's own fingerprint and the exact settings that produced it, so opening a second version of the same recording, or returning to a step you already ran, reuses the work rather than repeating it.

Access is a single policy, checked in a single place. Every page, audio file, pitch track and practice session passes the same gate, because an access rule reimplemented per surface is how a media library eventually leaks. Visibility and permission stay separate questions: a link is a way to find a lesson, never a way past a rule about who can open it. Recordings stay private until their owner publishes a lesson made from them; a lesson cannot be published while its content, its audio or its rights are unresolved; and nothing reaches the public catalogue before review, while direct links keep working while that review is pending - so approval gates discovery rather than access. Moderation is auditable by construction: each decision records who made it, why, and what changed. Operators can review public content and still cannot open anyone's private recordings.

It runs at [ragafied.com](https://ragafied.com). The whole production footprint is defined in code rather than assembled by hand - one small server carrying the site, the practice space, the studio and the short background jobs, separate scale-to-zero capacity for the audio work, and private storage for every recording - and a verified build on the main branch deploys itself. The database and the recordings are each copied to a second region, a deletion in one never propagates to the other, and there are written procedures for bringing back the database or the whole service. Storage is deliberate about what it keeps: a track's original and whatever the player needs stay, the working files that processing leaves behind are stored in compact lossless and lossy forms rather than raw audio, and anything that can be rebuilt - superseded separations, audition clips, the processing of a track abandoned before release - is cleared after a period of inactivity, with an expired track one click from being prepared again. Separation is no longer a spike that ran on a laptop; it processes real uploads, and long recordings are checked against recorded acceptance evidence rather than assumed to work. Every track carries its own processing workspace - stems, rhythm, pitch, phrases, review - with one timeline, one autosave and one undo across all of it, and each stage shows where it stands and names what it is still missing; an existing playable version stays available while a replacement is being made, or after one fails. Rhythm is found by a neural beat tracker run over the whole recording, and the app is careful about what that gives you: beats and likely measure starts, not a tala. Cycle grouping - a 2+2+3, a slow vilambit cycle - and the sam are labels you place and verify by ear, and re-running detection leaves them where you put them. Pitch is analyzed over the sung passages before phrases are proposed, and the contour it produces can be corrected by hand, a point or a whole line at a time, with every edit reversible. Separation settings are chosen by listening rather than by argument: a thirty-second passage from the middle of every new song is run through three configurations automatically, the results are compared side by side against the spectrogram, and only then is one applied to the whole recording. The choice is made per part rather than per model - the vocal from one separation and the accompaniment from another if that is what sounds right, while rhythm is read from the untouched original - and when a result is rejected, the reason is kept, so the presets that keep losing are retired instead of argued about. The operator console was built twice on purpose: an off-the-shelf record browser first, to find out what running the service actually asks for, then a purpose-built one - searchable, hierarchical, with bulk actions and audio you can play straight from a job - once that shape was clear.

## A face for it

The public page now opens on the player itself rather than a picture of it. The same practice player that runs inside the product is embedded directly in the homepage, playing a sample track chosen by the site's administrators, so the first thing a visitor does is press play, slow a phrase down and drop the key - and the page recolours around whichever player theme they pick. Below it, the page speaks to two readers in turn rather than averaging them: a learner is told what a turn feels like, and someone with a recording is told what it takes to turn it into a lesson. The product's line is "Sing what you love. Love how you sing." The copy is deliberately plain about what happens - a short phrase plays with its original accompaniment, the singer drops out, and you sing the same phrase over the instruments. Hear, sing, compare. The words follow the register of good documentation: direct, literal, never cute.

The mark that sits on all of it is called Passage: three waves rising through a rounded tile, drawn as vectors so the same artwork serves the site, the app icon, the email header and print without depending on an installed font, beside a clean sans-serif wordmark. Its palette is espresso ink on ivory paper with one amber accent, and a media kit - every size, every colourway, a brand guide - regenerates from one source so the brand cannot drift between the two applications.

It is aimed at one thing first. Hindustani classical and Indian film music are where listening and repeating have always been the method, so that is what the product commits to; the same studio-and-player model could later carry dance, language, or anything else learned by repeating after someone, but that is a direction rather than a claim. The visual language moves as one system across the public site, the practice space, the studio, the account and administration screens and the transactional emails - calm instrument surfaces, a single accent colour used only as a signal. The player itself comes in four looks - a pearl glass default, a pink, a deep emerald and a warm solar one - chosen with a tap, remembered on the device, and switched without interrupting what is playing; the compact player is drawn at real size rather than scaled down, so its settings stay readable. A track can also carry a look of its own: its owner drops in a background image and picks the colours while a live preview of the real player follows along, and the track then opens in that theme for whoever plays it, as one more swatch beside the four, without touching the theme they chose for everything else. The owner can save how the track plays best in the same place: the review step lists, in plain words, every player setting they changed from the defaults - balance, mode, tempo, key - each with a way to put it back, and saving that list means the track opens that way for anyone who plays it, while each singer's own later adjustments are still remembered for them alone.

## The 2012-13 build

The first attempt was a Rails 3.2 SaaS scaffold shaped around the same idea: `Raga`, `Guru`, `Lesson` and `Audio` models wired up alongside users and roles, with Devise and CanCan for auth, Bootstrap and Haml on the front end, and RSpec and Cucumber behind it. It is a fair snapshot of how a full Rails application got bootstrapped at the time, and of an idea that had to wait more than a decade for the browser to catch up with it. The 2026 version shares none of its code.
